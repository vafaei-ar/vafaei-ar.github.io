About

From physics to clinical AI

I am an Assistant Professor in the Department of Public Health Sciences at Penn State College of Medicine. I trained as a physicist and now work on applied machine learning in healthcare.

I collaborate with clinicians and data scientists to study what happens when models meet incomplete records, differences between hospitals, and practical constraints in patient care. My interests also include uncertainty, reproducible research, and the resources needed to run AI systems.

Research

Research areas

I focus on methods that can be evaluated, reproduced, and understood in clinical settings.

01

Clinical data quality

Missing-data imputation, cohort definitions, and EHR phenotypes across healthcare systems.

02

Reliable clinical prediction

Model evaluation under hospital differences, limited outcome labels, uncertainty, and calibration.

03

AI in patient care

Human-supervised tools for post-discharge communication, with attention to safety and workflow fit.

04

Efficient and sustainable AI

Environmental costs of AI training and inference, and comparisons that consider both performance and resource use.

EHR methods

Pympute

A Python toolkit for missing-data imputation, including electronic health records. The methods are described in a 2025 Scientific Reports paper.

Patient-facing AI · Research prototype

AI-SoNar / VERA Cloud

A voice-first stroke follow-up prototype for structured check-ins and human review. It uses synthetic test data. Its clinical escalation rules are drafts pending clinician approval.

Multisite EHR research

Computable stroke phenotypes

Code and aggregate benchmarking materials for comparing ischemic-stroke phenotype rules across health systems. Aggregate agreement does not establish patient-level accuracy.
